Ensemble Methods: Voting vs Stacking

Step 1 of 9

The Wisdom of the Crowd

In Machine Learning, relying on a single model can be risky. It might overfit, or it might just be bad at predicting certain types of data.

Ensemble Methods solve this by combining multiple models (called "base learners") to create one strong, highly accurate super-model. Today, we'll explore two crucial techniques: Voting and Stacking.

1 The Voting Classifier

Think of a Voting Classifier like a democratic election. You train several different independent models (e.g., a Decision Tree, an SVM, and a Logistic Regression) on your dataset.

When a new data point arrives, every model makes a prediction. The final output is decided by gathering their "votes".

Two Types of Voting:

  • Hard Voting: Majority wins. If 2 models say "Cat" and 1 says "Dog", the answer is Cat.
  • Soft Voting: Averages the probabilities. If models are 90%, 40%, and 60% sure it's a Cat, the average probability is 63% (Cat wins).

2 The Stacking Classifier

Stacking (Stacked Generalization) is the hierarchical, "smart" version of voting. Instead of blindly trusting a majority vote, Stacking learns which models to trust.

First, base models (Level-0) make their predictions. Instead of aggregating these immediately, Stacking uses these predictions as new input features to train a final "Meta-Model" (Level-1).

Why is this better?

The Meta-Model might learn: "When Random Forest says Class A, it's usually right. But when SVM says Class B, it's often wrong." It automatically figures out the optimal way to combine them!

The Math Behind the Magic

Hard Voting (Majority Rule)

The final prediction \( \hat{y} \) is the mode (most frequent prediction) among the \( m \) base models \( h_i(\mathbf{x}) \).

$$ \hat{y} = \text{mode} \{ h_1(\mathbf{x}), h_2(\mathbf{x}), \dots, h_m(\mathbf{x}) \} $$

Soft Voting (Weighted Probabilities)

Selects the class \( c \) that maximizes the average (or weighted) predicted probability \( P \) across all \( m \) models.

$$ \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(y=c \mid \mathbf{x}, M_i) $$

Stacking Classifier

First, calculate intermediate predictions \( Z \) from \( m \) base models. Then, pass \( Z \) to the meta-model \( f_{\text{meta}} \).

$$ Z_i = h_i(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, m $$ $$ \hat{y} = f_{\text{meta}}(Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_m) $$

Where \( f_{\text{meta}} \) is commonly a Logistic Regression model learning the optimal weights for \( Z \).

Real-World Application

The Scenario: Predicting Student Success

Imagine you are evaluating students for a data science project. You want to predict whether a specific student, let's call him Student A, will successfully pass (Class 1) or fail and need intervention (Class 0).

You have trained three base machine learning models. Here are their raw probability predictions that Student A will PASS:

Model 1 (\( h_1 \)) Random Forest
0.70
(70% confidence)
Model 2 (\( h_2 \)) SVM
0.40
(40% confidence)
Model 3 (\( h_3 \)) KNN
0.80
(80% confidence)

Let's see how our Ensemble techniques calculate the final grade for Student A.

Applying Voting Calculations

1. Hard Voting Calculation

Threshold = 0.5

First, convert probabilities into crisp class labels (0 or 1):

  • \( h_1(x) = 0.70 \implies \) 1 (Pass)
  • \( h_2(x) = 0.40 \implies \) 0 (Fail)
  • \( h_3(x) = 0.80 \implies \) 1 (Pass)

Apply the majority rule formula:

$$ \hat{y}_{hard} = \text{mode} \{ 1, 0, 1 \} = 1 $$
Conclusion: Hard Voting predicts Student A will PASS.

2. Soft Voting Calculation

Instead of deciding immediately, average the raw probabilities from all models:

$$ \hat{y}_{soft\_prob} = \frac{0.70 + 0.40 + 0.80}{3} $$ $$ \hat{y}_{soft\_prob} = \frac{1.90}{3} \approx 0.633 $$

Since \( 0.633 \ge 0.5 \), the final prediction is converted to Class 1.

Conclusion: Soft Voting predicts Student A will PASS (63.3% confidence).

Applying Stacking Calculation

3. Stacking with Logistic Regression Meta-Learner

The meta-learner doesn't just average; it applies learned weights (\( \beta \)) to each model's prediction based on how trustworthy they were during training.

Bias (\( \beta_0 \))
-1.5
Model 1 (\( \beta_1 \))
1.2
Model 2 (\( \beta_2 \))
-0.5
Model 3 (\( \beta_3 \))
2.0

Notice how Model 2 has a negative weight? The meta-learner learned that Model 2 is often wrong, so it actively penalizes it!

Step A: Calculate Log-Odds (\( z \))

$$ z = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 \times h_1) + (\beta_2 \times h_2) + (\beta_3 \times h_3) $$ $$ z = -1.5 + (1.2 \times 0.70) + (-0.5 \times 0.40) + (2.0 \times 0.80) $$ $$ z = -1.5 + 0.84 - 0.20 + 1.60 = \mathbf{0.74} $$

Step B: Convert via Sigmoid Function

$$ P(y=1) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$ $$ P(y=1) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.74}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.477} \approx \mathbf{0.677} $$
Stacking Conclusion: The meta-learner calculates a 67.7% probability, confidently predicting Student A will PASS. By understanding which models to trust, it achieved a mathematically optimized result rather than a blind average!
